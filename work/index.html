Finding Underperforming Pages: A Position-Adjusted CTR Opportunity Score

Sehrish Binte Asghar  ·  Lane: CTR / Engagement Opportunity Scoring  ·  Repository ↗
Abstract

Which pages on a search results page are visible but under-capturing clicks relative to similar pages, and can this be identified reliably enough to prioritize content review? Using FlyRank's warehouse data for a mid-panel month (March 2026), I computed each page's click-through-rate (CTR) gap against the expected CTR for its position tier and content type, built a baseline scoring rule from this gap weighted by traffic volume, and validated a Random Forest model against it using a page-grouped train/test split. The model did not outperform the simple baseline (MAE 0.00545 vs. 0.00542) — a modest, honest result showing that position tier and content type alone already capture most of the available signal. A separate audit of a related product assumption — that content staleness predicts CTR decline — found it did not hold on this data. Results are observed and directional, intended to support human review decisions, not causal claims about search ranking.

1. Introduction

Research question: which visible pages under-capture clicks relative to similar pages (same search position and content type), and can a model identify these opportunities more reliably than a hand-written baseline rule?

Decision this supports: which pages a content team should prioritize for metadata/title review, versus which pages are already performing as expected for their position. A ranked, confidence-aware queue lets a small review team focus limited time on the highest-value opportunities first, rather than reviewing pages in an arbitrary order or relying on raw CTR alone — which, as this project's early exploration showed, is misleading for low-sample content types and positions.

2. Data

Release: FlyRank ML Internship warehouse (Hugging Face: FlyRank/internship-warehouse).

Tables used:

  • fact_content_daily_performance — daily search performance per content item per client (impressions, clicks, average position).
  • dim_content — content-level attributes (content type, created/updated dates).

Date window: month=2026-03, a single mid-panel month, deliberately avoiding the sealed final month (June 2026), which the dataset reserves as a held-out window for any past-to-future label.

Excluded, and why (public-safe):

  • Traffic-channel and AI-referral breakdown columns — out of scope for a CTR/engagement lane, and mixing them in would blur what's actually driving the opportunity signal.
  • Scroll-event data — too sparse in this release to use reliably as a feature.
  • Rows without valid GSC data (~63% of the month) — no trustworthy position or CTR values.

No client names, domains, URLs, or raw queries appear anywhere in this work. All client and content identifiers are pseudonymized hashes provided by the release itself.

3. Methodology

Assumptions

  • CTR is comparable within the same position-tier + content-type group; a page's "expected" CTR is well-approximated by its group's behavior in the same month.
  • Position and content type are known before any click happens, so they are safe model inputs; raw CTR and clicks are not, since they are the outcome itself.
  • A single mid-panel month is sufficient to build and validate a baseline model; multi-month trend and persistence are out of scope for this capstone.

Features

Position tier (bucketed from average search position), content type, and log-scaled impression volume — all three knowable independent of the day's click outcome.

Label

CTR (clicks ÷ impressions) as a continuous regression target, computed only on rows with valid, available search-console data.

Baseline

Opportunity score = (group-mean CTR for position tier + content type − the page's own CTR) × log(impressions), with reason codes and a review-now / review-later action label.

Model

A Random Forest Regressor predicting CTR from position tier, content type, and log-impressions. The model's own prediction replaces the baseline's group mean as the "expected CTR" reference point.

Validation design

Grouped split by content item — not random — so the same page never appears in both train and test. A page's multiple report-days are near-duplicates; a random split would let the model see the same page's pattern during training, inflating its apparent performance.

Leakage checks

No raw CTR, clicks, or any label-derived column is used as a model input — confirmed against a deliberate leakage trap earlier in this project: feeding a label-derived gap column into a model drove its score to a perfect, meaningless 1.000; excluding it gave an honest, believable result instead. No future-window data is used; only the single mid-panel month. No internal product flags are used, since the release does not ship them.

The model does not outperform the baseline — its error is marginally higher, a difference small enough to be within noise rather than a meaningful improvement. This indicates the three features used don't carry more predictive information than a simple group average already captures. The baseline's group-mean approach is, on this evidence, an adequate and more interpretable choice for this task — added model complexity isn't currently earning its keep.

5. Limitations & honest framing

  • The model does not beat the baseline on this feature set and split — this work does not claim the model is production-ready or superior; it establishes a baseline and shows what more would be needed.
  • Single mid-panel month only — no trend or persistence data; a page's opportunity status here is a one-month snapshot, not a durable pattern.
  • No causal claims. A CTR gap indicates a page underperforms its peers, not why — it cannot distinguish a title problem from search-intent mismatch, seasonality, or consolidation with a sibling page.
  • Position tiers are a bucketing convention defined for this project; the raw warehouse ships continuous position, not pre-made tiers. A different bucketing choice could shift results.
  • Engagement data is sparse — only around 10% of rows have any session-engagement activity at all, limiting how far the "engagement" half of this lane could be explored with confidence in this pass.
  • All claims here are observed, directional, and decision-support only. Nothing in this paper proves how any search engine's ranking algorithm behaves, or guarantees a CTR improvement from acting on any recommendation.

6. Ranked recommendations

  1. Prioritize by position-adjusted CTR gap, not raw CTR. A page's CTR should always be judged against its position peers, never in isolation.
  2. Weight by volume, not raw gap severity. A large gap on a high-impression page has more real-world upside than the same gap on a low-impression page — review these first.
  3. Deduplicate by page before reviewing, not by page-day. A day-by-day ranked queue can surface the same handful of pages repeatedly; a reviewer should see one row per page.
  4. Don't use "days since last update" as a refresh-priority signal on its own. The staleness audit found this assumption doesn't hold on this data — combine it with position/CTR evidence, or drop it.
  5. Treat exact-zero CTR at high volume as a possible tracking issue, not only a content problem. Several flagged pages had suspiciously exact-zero CTR at large impression volume — worth a tracking sanity check before assuming it's purely a metadata fix.
  6. Don't over-invest in modeling complexity yet. The Random Forest did not beat the simple group-mean baseline on this feature set — rely on the simpler, more interpretable rule today, and revisit modeling once richer features (multi-month trend, better-covered engagement data) are available.
